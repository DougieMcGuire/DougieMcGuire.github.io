Four people build it, in this order.

The HR 1120 is hand-wound because a rotor would hide the bridges, and the bridges are the part we spend the most time on. Here is what happens between the raw brass and the box.

1

Cutting the bridges

Brass plate goes on the mill on a Monday. The bridge outlines, the jewel seats and the click spring all come off one setup, so nothing drifts between operations.

2

Anglage, by hand

Every bridge edge is filed to a 45-degree bevel and polished with a boxwood stick until it throws a hard line of light. This is the slowest stage: eleven hours a movement, and the reason we finish forty a year rather than four hundred.

3

First assembly, then apart again

The movement is built up, run for a week, then completely stripped. Assembly marks the finish, so the parts are re-polished and it is built a second time. You only ever see the second build.

4

Timing in five positions

Regulated over fourteen days, dial up, dial down, and on each of three crown positions. It leaves the bench inside two seconds a day. We record the numbers and send them with the watch.

Two references. That is the whole catalogue.

Both are 39mm and 8.4mm thick, so they sit under a shirt cuff. The difference is the dial and what it is for.

Nocturne 39

Salmon dial, small seconds, no date. Made for people who look at a watch to read the time and then stop looking at it. The dial is galvanic-treated brass, sunburst from the centre, and it shifts from copper to pink as you turn your wrist.
